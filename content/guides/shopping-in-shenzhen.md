Shenzhen is a good city to buy things in. The problem is that **the first shops a visitor sees are the worst value in the city**, and by the time you work that out you have already paid for a bottle of water at twice the normal price.

I live in Luohu, a few minutes from the border crossing. Here is what I'd tell a friend arriving for the first time.

## The Border Premium Is Real

Walk out of the Lo Wu crossing and you are immediately among restaurants with someone standing outside calling you in, people selling tour packages, and small shops selling SIM cards, charging cables and drinks.

**A bottle of water that costs ¥2–3 anywhere else in the city is ¥5 there.** The phone accessories are the same goods you'd find in Huaqiangbei at a noticeably higher price.

None of this is a scam. It's the ordinary economics of a captive audience: you have just crossed a border, you're carrying a bag, you have no sense of local prices yet, and everything is ¥5 so who cares. Multiply that by every purchase in your first hour.

**What to do instead:** take the metro two or three stops before you buy anything that isn't urgent. Water and snacks are cheapest from a chain convenience store or a supermarket, where prices are fixed and printed.

> There is a seven-floor mall right at the crossing, **Luohu Commercial City (罗湖商业城)**, that older guides still recommend for tailoring and knock-offs. **I have never been inside it, so I'm not going to review it.** What I can tell you is that everything in that immediate area prices itself the same way, for the same reason.

## Where to Actually Buy Things

| What you want | Where to go | Metro |
|---|---|---|
| Phone accessories, cables, chargers, repairs, second-hand phones | **Huaqiangbei** | Huaqiangbei, Lines 2 and 7 |
| Clothes | **Nanyou (南油)**, in Nanshan | Nanyou, Lines 9 and 12 |
| Water, snacks, toiletries, anything ordinary | Any chain convenience store or supermarket | Everywhere |

**Huaqiangbei** is the one genuinely world-class thing Shenzhen has for shoppers, and it's covered in detail in the [Huaqiangbei guide](/guides/huaqiangbei-electronics-guide) — including the fact that foreigners are routinely quoted two to three times the local price.

**Nanyou** is the clothing district. It's around **Nanyou station on Lines 9 and 12**, it runs roughly **10:00 to 19:00**, and it is enormous: several buildings and thousands of stalls. Two things to understand before you go:

- **It is a wholesale market first.** Buyers come to stock shops. Some stalls will sell you a single piece happily, some will price a single piece much higher, and some would rather not bother. That isn't personal.
- **Much of what's on sale is copied rather than licensed.** That is the local reputation of the district and it's part of why the prices work. See the section on customs below before you buy anything with a logo on it.

If you see **Dongmen (东门)** recommended elsewhere, it's a pedestrian street of street food and cheap fashion in Luohu — a good walk, and it's in the [three-day itinerary](/guides/3-day-shenzhen-itinerary).

## How Bargaining Actually Works

**First, check whether it applies.** Supermarkets, chain shops and mall stores have fixed prices, and trying to negotiate there is just awkward. Market stalls and small independent shops expect it.

**The method is unglamorous and it works:**

1. **Ask three stalls before buying anything.** In these markets the same item is sold by a dozen stalls in the same building. The first number you hear tells you almost nothing until you have heard three.
2. **Say it's too expensive and name a lower number.** Then work down in steps of twenty or thirty yuan, rather than jumping to your final price immediately.
3. **Be willing to leave.** The single most useful habit is not feeling obliged to buy from the stall you happen to be standing in. Walking out is completely normal here and nobody takes offence.

That last one matters more than any phrase. Most of the money visitors lose is not lost by bargaining badly — it's lost by deciding, halfway through a conversation, that leaving would be rude.

**How far can you push?** The Huaqiangbei guide has the concrete version: a phone case a local pays ¥30–40 for is quoted at ¥70–100 to a foreigner. The mechanism isn't dishonesty so much as arithmetic — ¥100 doesn't sound like much when you're converting from dollars in your head.

## Paying: The Thirty-Second Check

**Mobile payment is the default.** WeChat Pay and Alipay are what everyone uses, and many small stalls no longer keep much change for cash. Set one of them up with your foreign card [before you arrive](/guides/alipay-wechat-pay-setup).

**Some stalls take foreign cards.** Visa and Mastercard work at more places than they used to, but at a market stall it is worth asking first rather than assuming at the moment of payment.

**And then check the number.** Agree the final amount out loud before anything is scanned, look at the amount on the payment screen, and look at the notification on your phone afterwards. This is not paranoia about Shenzhen specifically — it is what I do anywhere the price was negotiated rather than printed, because an agreed price and a typed price are two different things.

## Touts, and Why They're a Signal

Around the crossing and in the busier markets, people will try to pull you into restaurants or sell you tours. Keep walking. A restaurant that needs someone standing outside to fill its tables is telling you something about the restaurant.

Nobody is going to hassle you for long. Declining and continuing to walk ends it.

## Fakes, and What Happens at the Border

If you buy a copy of a branded item, the thing to think about isn't the purchase — it's the journey home.

- **Hong Kong:** under the Trade Descriptions Ordinance, importing goods bearing a forged trademark is an offence, with penalties up to **HK$500,000 and five years' imprisonment**. If you're doing a day trip from Hong Kong, this is the border you cross with your shopping.
- **United States:** customs allows **one article of each type** bearing a counterfeit trademark, for personal use, not for sale.
- **Europe:** enforcement varies by country and France in particular is strict, with penalties aimed at buyers as well as sellers.

The practical version: a fake is not a bargain if it's confiscated, and rules differ enough by passport that "everyone does it" is not useful guidance. Check your own country's rules before you buy, not after.

## Common Questions

**Is Luohu Commercial City worth visiting?**
I haven't been inside, so I won't pretend to judge it. What I can say is that the area around the Lo Wu crossing charges border prices for ordinary things, and that the electronics sold there are cheaper in Huaqiangbei.

**Where do locals buy clothes in Shenzhen?**
Nanyou in Nanshan, around Nanyou station on Lines 9 and 12, is the clothing district. It's a wholesale market that also sells retail, open roughly 10:00 to 19:00.

**Do I have to bargain in Shenzhen?**
Only in markets and small independent shops. Supermarkets, chains and malls have fixed prices.

**Can I pay with a foreign credit card in Shenzhen markets?**
Sometimes, but ask before you commit. WeChat Pay and Alipay are what stalls expect, and both can be set up with a foreign card before you travel.

**Is it safe to shop in Shenzhen markets?**
Yes. The risks are commercial rather than physical: paying well over the local price, buying a second-hand phone with a problem, or being talked into a restaurant you didn't choose.
